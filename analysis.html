01Theory

Theory of Planned Behaviour

Firstly, Ajzen's (1991) Theory of Planned Behaviour suggests that behaviors are driven by intentions shaped by an individual's attitude, subjected norms, and perceived behavioural control. For example, the individuals from the chosen segment part take in higher education which raises more awareness on the environmental harm caused by fast fashion (Mobarak et al., 2025), so thrifting may seem like a more responsible option instead of a financial limitation for this segment. Not to mention, the community around them such as their peers, social media, and even campus events can make second-hand shopping socially approved with multiple resale apps and physical store branches. On the other hand, it is not that intention does not always lead to purchase. There may be consumers with strong pro-environmental mindset and attitudes but still choose to buy fast fashion due to the significantly low prices as well as trend pressures. This theory is able to explain why the chosen segment intends to thrift, but not entirely for its actual purchasing behaviour.

Source: Ajzen (1991); Mobarak et al. (2025)
02Theory

Self-concept

Secondly, the Self-concept theory suggests that individuals buy products that express who they are or who they wish to be (Sirgy, 1982), and what they own becomes an extension of their identity (Belk, 1988). This theory helps fill the gap from the Theory of Planned Behaviour. It is clear that fast fashion is mass-produced and identical for cost saving and speed, but it does not give customers a sense of individuality that a unique vintage or second-hand piece can. This is one of the reasons why thrifting now is not only for low-income consumers, but individuals from middle- and upper-class too (Halicki et al., 2024) but with a different motive. Low-income consumers may shop second-hand as a cheaper alternative, whereas middle-to-high-income consumers choose self-expression and status signaling. Although thrifting now shows ethical awareness, it may create a paradox as well. Because thrifting is cheap and thrilling, consumers may end up buying more as their identity motives may surpass the sustainability values they hold.

Source: Sirgy (1982); Belk (1988); Halicki et al. (2024)
03Theory

Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Thirdly, Maslow's (1943) Hierarchy can explain the chosen segment's behaviour through the level of needs that thrifting satisfies. Clothing is a basic need for everyone; thrifting meets it affordably whilst protecting financial security for young consumers such as students or professionals with limited incomes, linking this to the Safety Needs level. In some sense, thrifting also meets the Belonging needs where individuals can join peers and online communities that share the same value. Some examples of this may be sharing their finds on social media or even swapping tips on how or where to hunt for the best items. Higher up the chart, Esteem Needs are met through compliments and recognition (Halicki et al., 2024) for the specific thrifted pieces; this can include discontinued items from brands or even vintage items from the 2000s-90s. Lastly, Self-actualization can be met when consumers act on their values and choose to express their creativity whilst rejecting overconsumption. On the other hand, one weakness from Maslow's theory is that individuals are able to pursue higher needs without needing to satisfy the lower ones (Wahba & Bridwell, 1976). For example, a budget-conscious student can save money, feel part of a community, as well as act on their own values through a single purchase.

Source: Maslow (1943); Halicki et al. (2024); Wahba & Bridwell (1976)
